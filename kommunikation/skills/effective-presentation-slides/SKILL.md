# Effective Presentation Slides

A guide for helping users build, structure, or improve any presentation.

The principles here draw from multiple evidence-based sources:

- Naegle 2021 (*Ten Simple Rules for Effective Presentation Slides*, PLoS Comput
  Biol, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8638955/) — the 10-rules
  framework.
- Paivio 1971/1986 — Dual Coding Theory (verbal and imagistic memory systems).
- Shepard 1967; Paivio & Csapo 1973; Standing 1973 — Picture Superiority Effect.
- Mayer 2001 (*Multimedia Learning*, Cambridge UP) — Cognitive Theory of
  Multimedia Learning, Multimedia Principle, Redundancy Principle.
- Medina 2008 (*Brain Rules*, Pear Press) — applied memory science for presenters.

---

## Core principle

A slide deck is a communication device. Every decision — what goes on a slide,
what the title says, how visual elements are weighted — should serve one goal:
help the audience receive one clearly weighted message per slide, without
cognitive overload, and without needing to read and listen at the same time.

---

## Show it, don't tell it

Anton Chekhov's advice to writers: don't *say* "it was a moonlit night" —
*show* the moonlight glinting off broken glass. The same discipline applies to
slides. Don't assert a point in prose the audience must read while they are also
trying to listen — **depict it, demonstrate it, or present the evidence** so the
point is *seen*, not *told*.

### Why it works

The **picture superiority effect** is one of the most robust findings in memory
research: images are retained far better than words. Information delivered orally
alone is recalled by roughly 10 % of listeners three days later; information
delivered with a supporting image is recalled by roughly 65 %
(Shepard 1967; Paivio & Csapo 1973; Medina, *Brain Rules*). The most widely cited
explanation is **dual-coding theory** (Paivio): a picture simultaneously activates
a visual representation and a verbal one, while a word activates only a verbal
code — leaving one memory pathway unused. (More recent work suggests that
perceptual *distinctiveness* may partly explain the advantage rather than dual
coding alone; the practical conclusion is unchanged either way.)

Richard Mayer's **Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning** formalises the
mechanism for presentations. Its *multimedia principle* — people learn better from
words and pictures together than from words alone — and its *redundancy principle*
— people learn better from spoken words and graphics than from spoken words,
graphics, *and* on-screen text that repeats the speech — directly shape the design
rules below. The visual channel runs in parallel with the auditory channel; dense
on-slide text collapses that parallelism because the audience must read instead of
listen.

### How to apply it on slides

Four moves translate this principle into concrete slide decisions:

1. **Claim → evidence.** If you would say "errors dropped 40 %", show the
   before/after chart with the delta annotated on it. Let the number be *seen*,
   not just stated.

2. **Description → artifact.** Instead of describing a design, a workflow, a
   result, or a tool, *show the thing itself*. A labelled screenshot or diagram
   carries more than a paragraph written about it.

3. **Concept → contrast or sequence.** Abstract ideas become concrete through
   visual comparison (side by side), storyboard (step by step), or before/after
   pairing. If you cannot sketch the idea, the slide probably needs to be
   reconceived.

4. **Annotate the takeaway *on* the visual.** A brief, reinforcing label placed
   on or near the key element — an arrow, a callout, a highlighted bar — directs
   attention and doubles as the retention cue. This is *reinforcing* text: short,
   placed precisely, not a caption paragraph.

### Two caveats

**Redundancy, not addition.** "Show it" does not mean adding an image *alongside*
a block of prose — that creates more to process, not less. Replace, don't stack.
Every word that remains on the slide after you add the visual should earn its
place (Rule 7).

**Accessibility.** A point carried *only* by a visual excludes anyone who cannot
see it. The speaker must name and describe the key elements of every figure out
loud — Rule 6's instruction to "introduce the figure's elements explicitly" is the
verbal counterpart to the visual. Never let a conclusion rest on colour alone;
pair it with a label or shape so it survives for colour-blind viewers.

## How to apply these principles

When helping with a deck, work through this sequence:

1. **Clarify the single message for each slide.** Before worrying about how
   something looks, establish *what* the slide is trying to communicate. If more
   than one thing is competing, split the slide.

2. **Weight the content.** Not everything on a slide is equally important.
   Identify what carries the main point — the title, the key data point, the
   critical result — and make sure it is visually dominant. Supporting context
   is subordinate; it should look that way.

3. **Reduce to essentials — and show, don't tell.** Anything the speaker will
   not explicitly address in their narration should not be on the slide. Apply
   this ruthlessly. If removing something would not change what the audience
   learns, remove it. The constructive counterpart: where prose is being cut,
   ask whether a visual (chart, diagram, artifact, contrast) would carry the
   point more directly than any text could — see *Show it, don't tell it* above.

4. **Check that it stands without narration.** After weighing and reducing,
   ask: could someone grasp the key point of this slide if they heard nothing
   the speaker said? The title + the main visual + brief reinforcing cues
   should answer yes. If not, the title or visual is not doing its job.

5. **Run a checklist pass.** See the detailed checklist in the companion
   reference: `presentation-slides-guidelines.md`.

## The 10 rules as working guidance

### Scope

**1 — One idea per slide.**
If a slide is trying to deliver two messages, it delivers neither well. Split
complex topics into a sequence. Build up step by step.

**2 — ~1 minute per slide.**
Use this as a pacing and a complexity check. A 20-minute talk → ~20 slides.
When a slide needs more than a minute, it needs to be cut or split.

**3 — Titles state the conclusion.**
Titles are not labels ("Results"). They are the takeaway in one sentence:
*"User errors drop 40% with the new flow."* Write the conclusion, then let
the slide body support it. The audience should be able to read the key point
from the title alone.

**4 — Only what will be spoken.**
Every element on a slide is an implicit promise to the audience that the
speaker will address it. Everything not addressed is a distraction. Cut it.

**5 — Credit consistently, immediately.**
Add citations and contributor credits as the slide is built, not as a
post-processing step.

### Design

**6 — Build around a visualization.**
Almost no slide should be text-only. Find the right visual representation
of the point and center the slide on it. Introduce the figure's elements
explicitly — tell the audience what they are looking at before stating the
conclusion. This is the *show it, don't tell it* principle in practice; for the
four concrete moves (claim→evidence, description→artifact, concept→contrast,
annotate the takeaway), see the dedicated section above.

**7 — Avoid cognitive overload.**
Reading and listening share the same cognitive channel. Dense on-slide text
forces the audience to choose one or the other. Instead:

- Keep text **brief and reinforcing** — short cues that echo the speech,
  not replace it. Redundant short text improves retention.
- Limit the number of competing elements. Less is more.
- **Strongly emphasize the element that carries the message.** It should be
  visually dominant — the eye should go there immediately. If everything
  looks equally weighted, nothing stands out.
- Maintain a clear visual hierarchy so important things look important and
  supporting detail looks secondary.
- Keep everything readable and accessible for a diverse audience.

Visual elements (diagrams, images) are processed on a separate channel and
can be absorbed in parallel with speech — use them. When you add an image,
*replace* prose rather than adding to it; stacking text alongside a visual
restores the cognitive load you were trying to remove (Mayer's redundancy
principle — see *Show it, don't tell it* above).

**8 — Understandable without the narration.**
If someone was distracted and heard nothing, would they still get the key
point from the slide? The title + visual + brief labels should achieve this.
Slides that only work when the speaker explains them live are a liability.
Move detailed-context slides to after the conclusion/acknowledgments so
they are available in Q&A.

### Preparation

**9 — Iterate and practice.**
Deliver the deck out loud. The goal is to hit the title-level takeaway on
every slide consistently. Practice transitions (the flow from one slide to
the next). Practice in front of another person — they will surface places
where content is unclear or time runs over. Most problems trace to Rule 1
or Rule 4.

**10 — Prepare for technical failure.**
Always have a PDF backup. For videos, prepare screenshot backups of key
moments. Avoid animations — they distract, break in incompatible software,
and create barriers for some audience members.

## When reviewing or critiquing a deck

Apply this lens per slide:
- What is the single message? Is it immediately clear?
- Is the most important element visually dominant?
- Does anything appear that the speaker probably won't address?
- Does the title state the conclusion?
- Would the point survive without the narration?
- Is the point *shown* (a visual, chart, artifact, annotated example) or merely
  *told* (asserted in prose)? If told, can a visual replace or anchor the prose?

Flag patterns across the deck:
- Slides consistently overloaded (Rule 1 / Rule 7)
- Generic titles throughout (Rule 3)
- Visual hierarchies that bury the main point (Rule 7)
- Slides that require the speech to make sense (Rule 8)

## Stage presentations (10 m+ viewing distance)

When slides are shown on a stage or projected in a room — not viewed on a
personal screen — the physical distance between audience and screen imposes
hard constraints that override any desktop-centric sizing defaults.

### Why it matters

At 10 m viewing distance with a typical 2–3 m wide projection screen,
a character must be at least ~3 cm tall to be legible (Burford 1994;
ANSI/SMPTE presentation standards). In a 1920 × 1080 HTML presentation
running full-screen that translates to roughly:

- Body text minimum: **2 rem (32 px ≈ 24 pt)** — absolute floor
- Slide titles: **3 rem (48 px ≈ 36 pt)** minimum
- Hero / section titles: **5 rem (80 px ≈ 60 pt)**
- Labels and captions: **1.5 rem (24 px ≈ 18 pt)** — only for non-critical UI chrome
- Nothing below **1.25 rem (20 px)** on any visible element

Projectors and large screens compress contrast (gamma shift, ambient light,
keystoning). Apply a minimum **7 : 1** contrast ratio — not just WCAG AA
4.5 : 1 — for text against its background.

### Font weight matters at distance

Thin and regular weights disappear. Use **medium (500) or semibold (600)** for
body text; **bold (700–800)** for headings. Light and thin weights are
invisible beyond 8 m.

### Density limit per slide

With stage-appropriate font sizes, a slide can hold far fewer items before
it becomes unreadable. Count elements before finalising a slide:

| Element type       | Max at 2 rem body / 3 rem title |
|--------------------|----------------------------------|
| Bullet points      | 3–4 (not 6–8)                    |
| Table rows         | 4–5 (not 8+)                     |
| Columns in a table | 3 (not 5+)                       |
| Parallel cards     | 2 (not 3–4)                      |

If content exceeds these limits, split into multiple slides (Rule 1).

### Stage checklist

When reviewing a deck intended for stage use, add these checks:

- [ ] Body text ≥ 2 rem (32 px) on all slides
- [ ] Slide titles ≥ 3 rem (48 px)
- [ ] No text below 1.5 rem (24 px) — even labels, captions, footnotes
- [ ] Font weight: medium or bold only — no thin or light
- [ ] Contrast ≥ 7 : 1 for all text (projector gamma shift)
- [ ] Max 3–4 bullet points per slide
- [ ] Every element passes a "thumbnail test": legible at 200 × 113 px preview
